# Winning Strategy — Beyond Qualifying

> Internal document. Not for the team playbook PDF, not for the technical brief.

## The scoring surface: gate vs differentiator

Read the rules as two separate mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What it is | Who clears it |
|---|---|---|
| **>90% recall on Military/CEMA + Jamming** | A **gate**. Pass/fail | Every team in the top 10 |
| *"significantly higher technical scores by the expert panel"* | The **differentiator** | Where the ranking actually happens |

Hitting 90.1% and stopping puts you in the qualifying pool, not at the top of it.
The gate is table stakes; plan for it to be cleared by everyone you're competing
against.

## The single biggest differentiator: generalisation

Here is the thing most teams will get wrong.

They will synthesise training data, train on it, test on a split of *the same
synthetic data*, report 97%, and submit. Then the organisers' **Qualifier IQ
Stream** — generated by someone else, with different parameters — comes back at
60%.

**Our judged classes are synthetic (FHSS, jamming) or from a third-party dataset
(radar). None of them came from the organisers.** So the entire competition, for
us, reduces to one question: *does what we learned transfer to signals we have
never seen?*

Everything below serves that.

### 1. Domain randomisation — the highest-value change available

**Widen every parameter range beyond what feels realistic.** This is the standard
technique for sim-to-real transfer, and it is a config edit.

The logic: if our radar training data spans PRIs from 1–10 ms, and the
organisers' radar uses 4 ms, we are fine. If we tuned narrowly to 2–3 ms because
it "looked right", we fail. **A wider training distribution is more likely to
contain their signal.**

| Parameter | Current | Consider |
|---|---|---|
| `radar.pulse_width_s` | 10–100 µs | widen both ends |
| `radar.pri_s` | 1–10 ms | widen both ends |
| `fhss.hop_rate_hz` | 100–1000 | widen — real tactical radios span far wider |
| `jamming.jsr_db` | 0–20 dB | widen upward |

Constraint: **Nyquist still binds.** `tests/test_config.py` blocks anything that
would alias. Widen up to that limit, not past it.

Cost: one config edit and one retrain. Expected benefit: the largest of anything
on this list.

### 2. Prove generalisation — do not just claim it

Train on one parameter subset, evaluate on a **disjoint** subset. For example:
train on radar PRIs of 1–5 ms, test on 6–10 ms.

If accuracy holds, the model learned *"chirp"* rather than *"our specific chirp"*.
If it collapses, we found the fatal flaw ourselves — before the judges did.

Report it in the brief as a **held-out parameter generalisation test**. Almost no
student team will do this, and it directly answers the question a technical panel
is silently asking.

Cost: one extra training run. Value: very high, both technically and on the page.

### 3. Test-time augmentation — free accuracy

At inference, run each window several times under random phase rotations and
average the predictions. Phase rotation is label-preserving, so this is
mathematically free — it just cancels noise.

Typically worth a small but real gain, costs a few lines in `infer.py` and some
compute. Do it after the model is final.

### 4. Seed ensemble — reliable, cheap

Train the same architecture with 3 different random seeds and average their
outputs. Reliably worth a couple of points, requires zero new ideas, and training
runs are minutes on this model.

Only do this once a single model is clearing the gate. It is a polish step.

### 5. Overlapping inference windows

`infer.py --stride 512` instead of the default 1024. A burst straddling a window
boundary gets split and misclassified; overlapping windows catch it whole. Pure
compute cost, no risk.

## Target the "Competitive Advantage" clause explicitly

The rules name one thing specifically:

> *"Models that can successfully distinguish between standard communication
> signals and hostile CEMA interference (e.g., RF Jamming) will be awarded
> significantly higher technical scores."*

That is a **binary discrimination task**: comms vs jamming. So report it as one,
prominently, with its own headline number:

```
Communications vs Hostile CEMA discrimination:  99.2%
  (BPSK/QPSK/16QAM/64QAM  vs  JAMMING)
```

Most teams will bury this inside a 7-class confusion matrix and leave the panel
to find it. Putting it on its own line, using the rules' own vocabulary, makes
the scoring criterion trivially easy to award. Do not make judges work to find
the number they are looking for.

Same logic for **coarse-tier accuracy** (Civilian / Military / Hostile). It is
the false-alarm-rate story, it will be higher than the 7-class number, and it is
what actually matters operationally.

## The technical brief is scored — write it like it is

Phase 1 has five deliverables and only one of them is code. The brief is where a
panel forms its opinion.

### What reads as expertise

**An ablation table.** Retrain with one component removed at a time and tabulate
the result:

| Configuration | Radar recall | FHSS recall | Jamming recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full pipeline | — | — | — |
| − class weighting | — | — | — |
| − augmentation | — | — | — |
| − SNR sweep (high-SNR only) | — | — | — |

This is the most academically credible thing available for the cost. It proves
every design choice was measured rather than guessed. A handful of training runs.

**Naming limitations before the panel finds them.** FHSS and jamming are
synthetic, validated by internal-consistency tests rather than expert review.
Parameter ranges come from general literature, not a specific emitter. Say so.

A team that states its own limits reads as more credible than one claiming 99%
with no explanation — and a technical panel *will* probe an unexplained 99%.

**Explaining where the system stops and why.** We do detection and
classification. We do not do friend-or-foe, because that requires an ELINT
emitter library and IFF correlation — data no student team has lawful access to.
Saying this shows we understand the full EW pipeline rather than being unaware of
it.

**The Phase-2 roadmap, for free.** RadChar ships regression labels (pulse width,
PRI, pulse count, time delay) — which *are* an emitter fingerprint. State that the
architecture extends naturally to emitter characterisation. Costs a paragraph.

### What reads as inexperience

- Uniform ~99% with no accuracy-vs-SNR curve (implies leakage or a shortcut)
- No limitations section
- Architecture diagram with no justification for the choices
- Claiming friend-or-foe capability
- Overall accuracy quoted where the rules ask for per-class recall

## Video: the cheapest differentiator on the list

Five minutes, and most teams will submit a screen recording of someone reading
slides.

- **Everyone narrates their own category.** More convincing, easier to record.
- **Show the spectrograms.** This work is visual — a radar diagonal beside FHSS
  blocks beside barrage haze explains more in fifteen seconds than a paragraph.
- **Show inference running live** on the qualifier stream, with the log appearing.
- **Say the benchmark numbers out loud** while the scorecard is on screen.
- Rehearse once. The daily sync already is this rehearsal.

## Phase 2 preparation that costs nothing now

If we reach the top 10 there is a booth, a poster, a jury pitch and a **live
stress test on hidden data**.

Three things to do *now*, while they are free:

1. **Keep `infer.py` able to accept arbitrary files.** It already does. Never
   hardcode a path — during the live test the jury hands over a file we have
   never seen, and a hardcoded path is a public failure.
2. **Keep the spectrogram figures at poster resolution.** P3 and P4 are producing
   them anyway; save at high DPI now rather than regenerating under pressure.
3. **One line in the brief about the Phase-2 GUI** (real-time waterfall with
   threat highlighting). Shows we understand the full arc. Do not build it —
   the rules make it Phase 2 only.

## Time sinks that will not win anything

| Tempting | Why not |
|---|---|
| Transformer instead of CNN | Our bottleneck is data quality, not model capacity. Costs a day, changes little |
| Building a GUI now | Phase 2 only. Explicitly not scored in Phase 1 |
| Chasing 90% on 64QAM at −10 dB | Physically impossible — the constellation is inside the noise floor. Document the limit instead |
| Switching to spectrograms | Destroys phase, kills the civilian classes. Decided |
| TorchSig / Docker / GNU Radio setup | Does not cover our judged classes. Decided |
| Multi-label output | Correct in principle, needs full relabelling. Phase 2 |

## Priority order

Only start each once the previous is solid.

1. **Clear the gate** — >90% on all three judged classes
2. **Widen parameter randomisation** and retrain *(largest generalisation gain)*
3. **Held-out parameter generalisation test** *(largest credibility gain)*
4. **Comms-vs-jamming + coarse-tier headline metrics** *(targets the stated criterion)*
5. **Ablation table** *(brief quality)*
6. **Test-time augmentation, overlapping windows, seed ensemble** *(polish)*

Items 2 and 3 are where the ranking is won. Item 1 only qualifies us.
